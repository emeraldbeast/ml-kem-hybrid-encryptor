THE IDEA IN THREE MOVES

A KEM creates a shared key. It does not encrypt your message.

Think of X-Wing as a way for a sender and recipient to arrive at the same secret using two different mathematical routes.

Why does the paper leave ctM out of the final hash?

For this specific X-Wing construction, the paper's security analysis uses a property of ML-KEM ciphertexts to justify a shorter combiner input. The final SHA3-256 input is 134 bytes here. This is a construction-specific result; a generic hybrid KEM must not copy the omission without its own analysis.

The research comparison in this project adds the 1,088-byte ML-KEM ciphertext to the combiner input, bringing it to 1,222 bytes. That mode exists only to study the hashing cost and is not wire-compatible X-Wing.

WHERE THE PROJECT CAN GO NEXT

Research extensions with practical value

These ideas connect the construction to standards work and real deployment concerns.

A

Interoperability & known-answer vectors

Add deterministic draft vectors, malformed-input cases, and differential checks against an independent implementation. Keep paper-baseline vectors separate from draft vectors.

Cryptographic correctness
B

TLS migration & compatibility

Study a standardized hybrid TLS key share: handshake size, negotiation, middlebox failures, fallback behavior, and end-to-end latency. A KEM demo alone does not model a TLS deployment.

Protocol engineering
C

Measure parallel scaling honestly

Compare single operations with batches, report speedup and efficiency, and explain when OpenMP overhead outweighs the work. Keep the NTT experiment outside the vetted KEM path.
